Sustained-Release Drug Delivery for Neural Regeneration

Nerves heal slowly, so a drug that helps them regrow has to keep working for weeks. I helped characterize a polymer system that releases its drug gradually, and used mass spectrometry to show how long that drug stays stable.

Role
Research & development intern
Where
Axogen, Inc., Tampa
When
2024 – 2026
Methods
Release testing, tensile testing, LC-MS/MS, Python

This is proprietary industry research, so this page stays general. It describes methods and outcomes, not formulations or data, and the drawings are illustrations rather than Axogen images.

01Overview

When a peripheral nerve is injured, it regrows slowly, often around a millimeter a day, so recovery can take months. A drug that supports regeneration would need to be at the repair site for much of that time, and a single dose wears off long before the nerve has healed.

Axogen develops products for peripheral nerve repair. During my internship I worked on a polymer-based system designed to hold a drug and release it gradually, along with the testing that shows whether it works and how long it lasts.

02Objective

Two questions ran through the work. Does the system release its drug at a steady, predictable rate? And does the drug stay intact in storage long enough for the product to be useful?

03My contribution

  • Characterized the sustained-release system: how much drug it holds, how fast the drug comes out, and how the polymer holds up under tension.
  • Ran a six-month stability study comparing storage conditions.
  • Prepared samples for LC-MS/MS and analyzed thousands of results in Python and Excel.
  • Ran neurite outgrowth assays on dorsal root ganglion neurons to see how compounds affect nerve growth.
  • Wrote and revised more than 15 SOPs so every analyst prepared and tested samples the same way.

04Design process

The drug is held inside a polymer matrix. Instead of dissolving all at once, it diffuses out over time, and the way the polymer is processed changes how fast that happens.

For each version of the system I measured three things: how much drug it held (loading), how quickly the drug came out into solution (release kinetics), and how strong and stretchy the polymer was (tensile testing). Comparing them across processing conditions showed which conditions gave the most consistent release.

05Testing & validation

Every release and stability sample followed the same path: into a glass vial, through the LC-MS/MS, and onto a chromatogram where the drug's peak is measured. Scroll through it below.

In the stability study, samples kept under different storage conditions were pulled and measured on a schedule for six months. With thousands of results, ordinary run-to-run variation can look like degradation, so my analysis compared each change against the instrument's normal variability before calling it real.

06Results

6 monthsstability study comparing storage conditions
~20%longer shelf life from the storage conditions it set
5,000+mass spectrometry samples analyzed

The stability study defined storage conditions that extended the product's shelf life by about a fifth, and the release testing pointed to processing conditions that made release more consistent. Both fed into formulation and storage decisions.

07Key takeaways

  • Know your noise first. Before trusting a trend across thousands of results, I learned to ask how much the same sample varies on its own.
  • Release and mechanics are linked. Processing that makes the polymer stronger can also change how it releases, so the two have to be tested together.
  • Procedures make data comparable. A six-month study only works if the samples in month six were prepared exactly like the ones in week one.
