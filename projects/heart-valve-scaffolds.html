Electrospun Biomaterials for Heart Valve Tissue Engineering

Replacement heart valves can't grow or repair themselves, which matters most for children. I make electrospun scaffolds that cells could turn into living valve tissue, and test how their fiber structure shapes the way they stretch.

Role
Graduate assistant, M.S. thesis research
Lab
Translational Tissue Engineering & Biofabrication Lab
When
2026 – present
Methods
Electrospinning, SEM, FTIR, Raman, AFM, contact angle, biaxial testing

01Overview

When a heart valve fails, it is usually replaced with a mechanical valve, which needs lifelong blood thinners, or a valve made from animal tissue, which wears out over time. Neither can grow, so children often need repeat surgeries as they get older.

A tissue-engineered valve starts as a scaffold that the patient's own cells can grow into. Over time it could become living tissue that grows and repairs itself. Whether that works depends a lot on the scaffold: its fibers set how it moves, how strong it is and how easily cells can get in.

02Objective

Make scaffolds whose fiber structure behaves like native valve tissue, and make them consistently, batch after batch, so that structure and mechanics can be tuned on purpose rather than by luck.

03My contribution

  • Prepare polymer solutions and fabricate scaffolds by electrospinning.
  • Tune process settings to control fiber morphology, such as fiber diameter and alignment.
  • Check each batch for consistency with SEM, FTIR and biaxial mechanical testing, and characterize the scaffolds further with Raman, AFM and contact angle.
  • Relate the scaffold's microstructure to how it performs mechanically.

04Design process

Valve leaflets are stiffer in one direction than the other, which lets them open easily and still seal tightly. A scaffold has to come close to that while staying open enough for cells to move in. Electrospinning makes this possible, because the fibers' size, direction and density all come from the process. Scroll through it below.

Material and solution

The polymer and how much of it is dissolved set how thick the fibers come out and how the scaffold degrades.

Spinning settings

Voltage, flow rate, distance and collector speed control how stable the jet is, how thin the fibers get and how well they line up.

05Testing & validation

Each batch is checked for consistency, so that differences between scaffolds come from the design and not from the day they were made. Together, these methods describe a scaffold from its chemistry up to its mechanics.

  • SEM images measure fiber diameter and alignment.
  • FTIR and Raman confirm the scaffold's chemical makeup.
  • AFM maps the texture of the fiber surface.
  • Contact angle shows how easily the surface wets, which affects how cells attach.
  • Biaxial testing stretches the scaffold in two directions at once, the way a leaflet is loaded in a beating heart.

06Results

This is my active thesis research, so the findings aren't published yet. The questions it is working toward:

Process to structure

How changes in the spinning settings show up in fiber diameter, alignment and spacing.

Structure to mechanics

How that structure decides the way the scaffold stretches, and how close it gets to native valve tissue.

07Key takeaways so far

  • Consistency comes first. Before two designs can be compared, each one has to be reproducible.
  • Structure explains mechanics. SEM and biaxial testing tell the story together: what the fibers look like, and what that means for how the scaffold moves.
  • Small settings, big changes. A small shift in the process can change the fibers enough to change how the whole scaffold behaves.
